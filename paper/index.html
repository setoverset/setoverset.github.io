1. The 26-Letter Thing (Portfolio Architecture, Sort Of)

Here's the shape of it: I'm capping myself at 26 concurrent-or-sequential experiments, Sets A through Z, because apparently I needed an artificial ceiling or I would just keep spinning up new ideas until the heat death of the universe. The alphabet is doing me a favor here.

Set A is the money. Not "a" money — the money. Set A's entire job is to become a permanent, compounding capital reservoir that can fully fund and cash-flow everything else on this list, Sets B through Z, without me ever having to go hat-in-hand to a bank or an investor. Set A carries the whole building on its back.

Sets B–Z are the "think-and-do tank" — decentralized nodes poking at macroeconomics, behavior, and technology, all in service of the very unglamorous goal of helping humans understand each other a little better and move a little more value around a little more fairly. Grand, I know. I contain multitudes, apparently.

How Set A Actually Makes Money: Trading Around a Core

I want to be upfront that I'm explicitly not doing the "YOLO it all into one ticker and pray" retail thing, nor am I pretending to be a hedge fund. It's a dual-engine setup:

The Macro Engine holds; the Micro Engine trades.

The Macro Engine is the "I believe in this and I'm not going to flinch every time it dips 8%" part of me. The Micro Engine is the "okay but I'm also going to poke at the short-term chaos for cash flow" part of me. Both parts get to exist. Neither one is allowed to run the whole show.

2. The $250 Bullet (Options Sandbox Playbook)

I'm borrowing Jim Collins' "Fire Bullets, Then Cannonballs" idea here — you don't bet the farm on your first swing, you fire a cheap bullet, see where it lands, then decide if it's worth a cannonball. The $250 account is my bullet. It's calibrated to be small enough that I genuinely don't care if I lose all of it, which — paradoxically — is exactly what makes it a good teacher.

The rules I've set for myself, and actually stick to:

  • Cash account only, no exceptions. This isn't a technicality — it sidesteps Pattern Day Trader restrictions via T+1 settlement, sure, but more importantly it's a physical wall against overtrading. If the cash isn't settled, I literally cannot make the impulsive trade. My own plumbing protects me from myself.
  • Everything gets closed before Friday expiration, full stop. I'm not leaving anything open to find out what "broker auto-exercise" means the hard way. If I haven't closed it by Thursday night, that's a me-problem I fix immediately, not a Friday-morning surprise.
  • Stick to liquid chains. I've learned (the annoying way, probably) that thin option chains on penny-stock names — looking at you, GETY — turn into instant slippage losses the second you try to get out. PLTR, SOFI, GME: tight spreads, market makers actually show up. That's where I play.
  • Respect the IV Tax. Long-dated LEAPS come with a brutal time-decay surcharge, and high implied volatility environments can absolutely gut a position through IV crush even if I'm directionally right. So I lean toward shorter-dated, defined-risk vertical spreads — 30 to 60 days out — where the time-decay bill on day one is as small as I can reasonably make it.

3. Set B: The "Viral Value" Idea

Okay, this is the one that sounds the most like science fiction, so bear with me.

The premise: our current tax-and-redistribute system is slow, bureaucratic, and leaks value at every institutional joint along the way. What if wealth could move the way a virus moves — passively, locally, in real time, based on actual physical proximity to need, instead of waiting for a form to clear a government office?

Picture it like this:

Wealth moving like a virus — passively, locally, proximity-triggered.

The mechanics, stripped of the jargon:

  1. People are just people moving through space — tracked (securely, consensually) via phone or wearable.
  2. Everyone's got a wallet — a decentralized crypto wallet acting as a real-time snapshot of their financial "load."
  3. Proximity does the work — mesh networks, Bluetooth LE, even existing toll-style infrastructure, quietly noticing when a high-balance wallet passes near a low-balance one.
  4. Value sheds automatically, in tiny fractions, algorithmically, the way a wealthy commuter driving past an underserved neighborhood might — except instead of just driving past, a sliver of value actually moves.

I'll be honest, I don't have this fully solved — the privacy questions alone could fill their own whitepaper — but the core itch I'm scratching is: why does helping people who are physically near you have to be this hard and this slow?

4. Set C: The Chipotle Equilibrium

This one started as a joke and I liked it too much to let go.

The problem: depression and anxiety often show up as inertia — the "I know I should get up and move but I simply will not" flavor of bad day. Meanwhile, a burrito chain three blocks away has a walk-in traffic problem it needs to solve by 3pm to hit its quarterly numbers. These two problems are, weirdly, solvable by each other.

Here's the loop:

  • Your AI, on your side, quietly notices you're behind on your movement goal for the day and that this is the kind of day where a nudge actually helps.
  • Their AI, on the corporate side, notices foot traffic is soft and they need to spark sales before close.
  • The two systems talk to each other, in milliseconds, and the store issues you a hyper-targeted, time-limited offer — 50% off a burrito bowl — unlocked specifically by your phone or wearable confirming you actually walked the five miles.

Everybody wins in a way that doesn't feel like charity or manipulation:

  1. You get a little dopamine and a burrito bowl for breaking out of the couch-shaped dent you were in.
  2. The corporation gets a sale that genuinely wouldn't have existed otherwise.
  3. The community, in aggregate, ends up a little healthier and a little cheaper to take care of long-term.

Is it a little dystopian-sounding if I describe it wrong? Sure. Is it also just... a really good coupon with a pedometer attached? Also sure. I contain multitudes, again.

5. How I Actually Think About All This (The Bug Book)

I try to look at my own brain the way a scientist looks at a bug under glass — genuinely curious, zero judgment. ADHD hyperfocus, dopamine-chasing, depressive stretches, anxiety spikes, a rough trading day where I clearly let emotion drive — none of that is a character flaw I need to feel bad about. It's just data. An instance of Homo sapiens doing what that instance does, being observed so the system around it can get better.

A few borrowed mental models that actually stick for me, all from the poker table:

  • Expected Value (EV) over everything. Every trade, every allocation, every bet I place on an idea — I try to run it through long-term math, not the emotional weather of the last five minutes.
  • "Set Mining." Hunting for the deeply out-of-favor, overlooked ticker or entry point where a small, defined risk is quietly sitting on a much bigger, hidden payoff.
  • No intellectual vanity, ever. This one's borrowed from watching Michael Burry close Scion and, without ceremony, go build Cassandra Unchained instead. The second reality shifts underneath an idea — say, GME dilution genuinely breaking the mechanics of a squeeze thesis — I owe it to myself to drop the attachment immediately, cut exposure, and rotate. Being "right" about yesterday's thesis is worth exactly nothing if today's facts have changed.
